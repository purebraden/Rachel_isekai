# Chapter 21 — Observation

Rachel knew he was going to speak before he did, which wasn’t intuition
so much as the way attention has weight if you let yourself feel it. The
eastern hinge had already settled back into quiet — a small correction,
nothing dramatic, the kind of fracture that would have gone unnoticed by
anyone not trained to watch for the faint shimmer against sky — and she
should have stepped away. That was the rhythm. Correct, withdraw, let
the marker line resume its ordinary pretense of stability.

But she didn’t.

She stayed there a moment longer than necessary, palms still warm, heat
threaded thinly up her forearms and into her shoulders in that steady,
familiar way that no longer startled her but still didn’t feel normal
either, and she could feel him behind her. Not hovering. Not fidgeting.
Just fixed.

Watching her hands.

“Walker Rachel,” he said finally, and the formality alone was enough to
make something in her want to wince. “May I ask you something?”

She pressed her thumb against the base of her opposite palm, grounding
herself in the residual heat, and only then turned halfway toward him.

“You can,” she said. “I might not be able to answer.”

There was a flicker in his expression — not uncertainty. Anticipation.

“I want to understand your rotation.”

That word again.

Rotation, as though it were a maneuver one could diagram, as though
there were steps, as though it were a matter of precision and repetition
rather than whatever it actually was, which still refused to hold still
long enough to be named.

She faced him fully now. Beyond him, across the marker line, Arathen had
stilled in that quiet way of his that wasn’t interference and wasn’t
withdrawal either. Just attention without pressure. Sivara’s pen had
stopped moving — not dramatically, just the absence of the faint
scratching sound that usually accompanied field corrections.

“Rotation,” Rachel repeated, buying time.

“The riverbank hinge,” Caelen said quickly, words gathering momentum now
that they’d begun. “And the courtyard correction. You didn’t brace. You
didn’t reinforce. You changed the angle.”

He said it with conviction, as though stating a theorem he’d already
proven.

“I don’t change the angle,” she said, even though she knew that wasn’t
entirely true. “I—”

She faltered, irritated with herself before she’d even tried.

“I correct it.”

“That’s what I mean.”

“No,” she said, more sharply than she intended. “It isn’t.”

The wind shifted through the ward ribbons strung between the stones,
setting them whispering against each other. Caelen’s notebook was tucked
against his chest, edges bent from overuse. She wondered how many times
her hands were sketched inside it.

“I want to train under you,” he said.

It landed heavier than she expected. Not because she hadn’t seen it
coming — she had, for weeks now — but because hearing it aloud made
something in her tighten.

“I’m not a master,” she said immediately. “Sivara—”

“I’m not replacing her.” He caught the defensiveness in his own tone and
corrected it mid-breath. “I want to understand what you’re doing.”

Sivara closed her ledger with a soft, deliberate motion and regarded
them both.

“Walker Rachel,” she said, voice even and measured, “do you believe your
method is replicable?”

“I don’t even know what it is,” Rachel said finally.

“That isn’t possible,” Caelen said, frustration edging into his
composure. “You hesitate before you move. You measure something.”

“I don’t measure,” she snapped. “I listen.”

“To what?”

That stopped her.

“I don’t know,” she said, softer now.

Three days later, the southern walkway fractured midmorning while half
the town was moving through it.

Rachel felt it before she heard it.

“South line,” she said, already moving.

The hinge was unstable — skewed more aggressively than usual.

Caelen was already there.

“I see it,” he said.

“Caelen—”

He extended his hand.

He hesitated.

He tried to listen.

For a heartbeat, nothing happened.

Then the hinge tightened.

“Stop,” she said sharply.

The seam vibrated.

“Withdraw,” Sivara commanded.

He pulled back.

The fracture wavered in a more dangerous alignment than before.

Rachel stepped forward without thinking, warmth flaring down both arms.

She listened.

She rotated.

The fracture shuddered and then eased.

Silence pressed in.

“I was close,” Caelen said.

“No,” she answered.

And she knew next time he would not wait for permission.
