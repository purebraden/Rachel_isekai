# Chapter 22 — The Attempt

The seam surfaced while the square was still arguing about pears.

Rachel would remember that part later—the ordinariness of it. A woman
leaning over a stall insisting she’d paid less last week. Two children
circling the fountain in a game that involved more running than rules.
The fountain spilling water in a steady, unbothered arc.

The warmth tightened in her chest before the shimmer appeared.

“It’s opening,” she said.

Arathen turned at once. He didn’t look at the ground first. He looked at
her.

The distortion cut across the cobbles near the fountain, faint at first,
then widening in a diagonal that didn’t sit correctly. It wasn’t a
shallow street seam. It had weight to it, pressure beneath the surface
that pushed outward instead of settling inward.

Ilyra saw it too. “Back from the fountain,” she called, already moving
toward the nearest cluster of civilians. “Give them space.”

People obeyed in uneven waves. A basket tipped. Someone grabbed a child
by the shoulder and steered him toward the bakery steps. The square
loosened and tightened at the same time—bodies withdrawing, attention
narrowing.

Sivara stepped forward slowly, eyes fixed on the shimmer. “That’s deeper
than it looks.”

Rachel didn’t answer. She could feel the hinge grinding against itself,
the angle mis-set in a way that made her teeth ache. It wasn’t just
strained. Something had forced it slightly past where it wanted to rest.

Two apprentices moved in automatically, hands shaping reinforcement
along the seam’s edge. No one had to tell them. This was what you did.

The lattice tightened.

The hinge twisted.

Rachel felt the shift in her spine before she saw it in the stone. The
correction was pushing against the wrong layer. It wasn’t relieving
pressure—it was feeding it.

“Wait,” she said sharply.

The seam responded before the apprentices could.

It snapped inward and flared outward in the same breath, widening along
the cobbles with a violent shimmer that tore the lattice apart as if it
had been thread instead of structure. A crack shot through the stone
beside the fountain.

Gasps rippled through the square.

And inside the destabilizing field, too close to the center of it,
Caelen stepped forward.

Rachel saw him out of the corner of her eye—focused, pale, convinced.

“I see it,” he said.

“Caelen,” Sivara warned.

He extended his hand toward the seam.

He hesitated.

It was the pause he’d studied. The careful suspension before she moved.
He was trying to find the catch, the place where the hinge sat wrong.

For one fragile heartbeat, the seam seemed to hold.

Then it shifted.

Not yielding.

Inverting.

The angle skewed deeper than before, and the fracture widened with a
violent surge that knocked one apprentice backward. A visible ripple
raced toward the southern buildings, splitting across the base of a
support column with a crack that sounded too much like bone.

Someone screamed.

“I almost—” Caelen started.

The ground lurched beneath him.

The fracture branched, three new hinge points spidering outward in rapid
succession, each misaligned in a slightly different direction. Arathen
and Sivara both drove reinforcement inward again on instinct.

It made it worse.

The seam twisted under pressure, resisting force the way a locked joint
resists being forced straight.

“Stop pushing it!” Rachel shouted, though she wasn’t sure anyone heard
her.

The destabilization field collapsed inward around Caelen, compressing
space by a pace and snapping him off balance. He cried out, sharp and
startled.

Rachel stepped forward.

The warmth didn’t flare.

It dropped.

It dropped through her like a stone sinking through water, pulling
everything else with it.

The world arrested.

The scream hanging in the air stretched thin and unfinished. The flock
of gray birds lifting from the southern roofline froze mid-beat, wings
half-open, feathers trembling but not descending. Water arced from the
fountain basin and remained there in a curved sheet that refused to
fall.

The support column that had begun to tip stayed tipped.

Dust hovered in pale suspension.

Caelen hung between balance and impact, body tilted, arm twisting
beneath him without completing the motion.

Rachel moved.

Not fast.

There was no need for speed.

She stepped past Caelen’s suspended form and reached for the first hinge
without touching it. In the stillness, the misalignment felt louder,
vibrating against her bones. She slid the angle back into place with
careful pressure, guiding it into alignment.

The second hinge resisted, locked tight against its own strain.

She traced deeper beneath the visible fracture to the axis point that
had been forced off-center and turned that instead. The resistance
eased.

The third tried to shear outward.

She steadied it and rotated.

The fourth trembled, fighting for momentum that no longer existed.

She denied it.

Beneath all of them, she felt the original tilt—the place where the
pressure had first been nudged wrong—and she adjusted that cleanly.

The strain stopped.

Rachel exhaled.

The world resumed.

Sound returned violently. The suspended scream finished itself. The
birds snapped forward in chaotic motion and scattered across the sky.
The fountain water crashed down in a heavy slap. The half-fallen pillar
slammed fully into place with a jarring crack that split its surface but
did not bring it down.

Caelen hit the cobbles.

The impact tore the breath from him. His arm bent beneath him at a wrong
angle before he rolled onto his side, clutching it instinctively.

Dust fell all at once.

For a moment no one moved.

Then everyone did.

“Gods—”

“I couldn’t—”

“Did you see—”

“My hands wouldn’t—”

“It was the water—”

“Get back, give them air—”

Voices tangled and overlapped. Someone laughed once, high and
disbelieving, then went quiet. A woman sank down onto the bakery step
without looking, eyes still fixed on the sky.

Rachel swayed.

A hand closed around her arm before she realized she was leaning.

“Rachel.”

Arathen’s voice was close, steady in a way that cost him something.

“I’m here,” he said.

She nodded, though the warmth inside her was receding unevenly, leaving
her lightheaded.

Ilyra was already cutting a path toward Caelen. “Back. If you’re not
helping, step back.”

Sivara knelt beside him, fingers precise and unhurried despite the
tremor running through the square. “Don’t try to straighten it. Look at
me.”

“I thought—” Caelen’s voice broke.

Rachel moved toward him. Arathen’s hand fell away, but he stayed close.

Dust continued drifting from the cracked pillar in a fine, slow fall.

“It wasn’t like before,” someone said.

“I couldn’t move.”

“The birds—”

“It felt like—”

“No, it didn’t feel like anything—”

The words overlapped and dissolved.

Vaelis stood a few paces away, ink streaked across his hand where his
pen had dragged too hard when everything resumed. He wasn’t writing now.

He was watching her.

Rachel knelt beside Caelen.

His face had gone pale beneath the dust. Blood tracked from his temple
into his hair. His eyes found hers, unfocused, searching.

“I almost had it,” he whispered.

The square was still breathing unevenly around them—people shifting,
murmuring, staring at the pillar, at the fountain, at the sky as though
expecting it to misbehave again.

Rachel swallowed.

“I know.”

Behind her, the cracked pillar gave a faint settling creak.

No one laughed.

No one cheered.

They just kept looking.
