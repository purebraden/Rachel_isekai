# Chapter 23 — After

By dusk the square looked almost normal, and that was almost worse than
if it had remained broken.

The cracked pillar had been braced with timber supports that didn’t
quite match the stone, the fresh wood pale against the older masonry.
The spidering fracture near the fountain had been sealed over, though a
faint line still caught the light if you knew to look for it. Water
flowed again in its steady arc, spilling into the basin with the same
rhythm it had carried for years.

Rachel stood near the fountain longer than she needed to, palms resting
against the cool edge as if the stone might offer some kind of answer.

People crossed the square in small adjustments, not avoiding it
outright, but giving it space in a way they hadn’t that morning. And
when they saw her, their movement shifted again—subtle, but noticeable
once she’d seen it once and couldn’t unsee it.

“Rachel.”

She turned at the sound of her name, and something in her tightened
before she even registered who it was.

Tamsen stood a few paces away, flour still dusting her sleeves though
the bakery would have closed some time ago. She looked like someone who
had been deciding whether to approach and had finally chosen to do it
before she could reconsider.

“You saved us,” Tamsen said, and the words were steady, but not easy.

Rachel inclined her head slightly. “It didn’t look like that for a
moment.”

Tamsen let out a breath that hovered somewhere between a laugh and a
sigh. “No,” she admitted. “It didn’t.”

The silence that followed wasn’t empty; it carried the weight of
everything that hadn’t yet been said.

“My nephew was near the fountain,” Tamsen added after a moment, her gaze
drifting toward the repaired stone. “When it… changed.”

She didn’t say stopped. She didn’t need to.

Rachel felt the warmth inside her flicker, low and uneven.

“Is he hurt?”

“No. No, he’s fine.” The reassurance came quickly, then softened. “Just
shaken. He keeps asking about it, though.”

“About what?”

Tamsen hesitated longer this time, as if the word itself required care.

“The birds,” she said finally. “He said they stayed. Just stayed there,
like someone had pinned them to the sky.”

Rachel’s eyes lifted instinctively, though the sky above them now held
only the first thin scatter of stars.

“I didn’t mean for that to happen,” she said, and heard how insufficient
it sounded.

“I know.” Tamsen nodded, but the nod felt deliberate. “Of course you
didn’t.”

Another stretch of quiet. Someone crossed behind them, boots scuffing
against stone. The fountain continued its steady spill, indifferent.

“He asked how you did it,” Tamsen went on, her fingers tightening
briefly against each other. “I didn’t know what to tell him. I wasn’t
sure there were words that would make it smaller.”

Rachel swallowed.

“I don’t know what to tell him either,” she admitted.

That seemed to settle something—not comfortably, but firmly.

“We’ll keep him closer,” Tamsen said after a moment. “For a while. Just
until we… understand it better.”

The phrasing lodged beneath Rachel’s ribs.

“For a while,” she repeated quietly.

Tamsen nodded again, though her gaze had already shifted toward where
her nephew lingered near the bakery wall. “You did what you had to,” she
said, and this time the gratitude was unmistakable. “We are grateful.”

Rachel inclined her head once more, unsure what else to offer that
wouldn’t fracture further.

Tamsen lingered as though she might add something—an apology, perhaps,
or reassurance—but whatever it was didn’t come. She turned instead,
calling her nephew’s name before he could wander too far, and when he
reached her she caught his hand—not urgently, not roughly, just firmly
enough that he didn’t pull away.

Rachel watched them go.

Behind her, a door closed. Not slammed. Just shut with quiet finality.

“They’re trying to make it fit,” Arathen said softly.

She hadn’t heard him approach, but she wasn’t surprised he was there.
She didn’t turn immediately; she let the words settle first.

“Make what fit?” she asked.

“You,” he replied, and there was no accusation in it. Only observation.

She let out a faint breath that almost carried humor. “I was standing in
the same place I always stand.”

“That isn’t what I meant.”

She folded her arms loosely, more for the feeling of containment than
for warmth. “They thanked me,” she said after a moment. “And then told
me they’d keep their children closer. Not angrily. Not accusingly. Just…
carefully.”

Arathen considered that without rushing to soften it. “Yes,” he said.
“Careful.”

“That’s not how it was before.”

“No,” he agreed, and the agreement carried more weight than
contradiction would have.

A lantern flared to life near the southern wall, its flame catching and
wavering before steadying.

“Maybe I shouldn’t be here,” Rachel said at last, the thought emerging
more slowly than she’d expected. It didn’t feel dramatic. It felt
practical.

Arathen didn’t react immediately. He stepped closer, not touching her
yet, but near enough that she could feel the warmth of him at her side.

“Because of today?” he asked.

“Because of all of it.” She exhaled, searching for the shape of what she
meant. “Caelen nearly broke himself trying to do what I do. The
reinforcement failed. The seam twisted when he tried to copy the pause,
the angle, whatever it is I’m doing differently. And I can’t tell anyone
how to do it safely, because I don’t know what it is.”

“You didn’t ask him to try,” Arathen said quietly.

“No, but I showed him it could be done. That’s not nothing.”

He was silent for a moment, watching the braced pillar rather than her.
“If you hadn’t been here,” he said carefully, “that fracture would have
reached the southern buildings. It might have taken half the square with
it. There wouldn’t have been time for a second attempt.”

“You don’t know that.”

“No,” he admitted. “I don’t. But I know what I felt when the
reinforcement failed. And I know what I felt when it stopped responding
to force.”

She glanced at him sharply at the word, but he continued.

“When everything froze,” he said, “I was trying to reach you. I couldn’t
move. I couldn’t even shout properly. I don’t know what you did—but I
know that whatever it was, it wasn’t meant to harm us.”

She let that sit between them.

“When it happened,” she said slowly, “I didn’t feel powerful. I didn’t
feel in control. I felt like I was the only thing still moving in a
world that had decided not to. That isn’t a comforting feeling.”

“No,” he agreed softly. “It wouldn’t be.”

She pressed her palms more firmly against the fountain’s edge, grounding
herself in the chill of the stone. “I don’t know what I am becoming,”
she said. “And I don’t know how much further it goes. If this keeps
changing, if it keeps… widening…”

He stepped closer then, close enough that their shoulders touched.

“Then we learn it,” he said. “We don’t pretend it’s smaller than it is.
And we don’t decide you’re a danger because people are afraid of what
they saw.”

She let out a breath that trembled more than she liked. “I don’t want to
be something they have to be careful around.”

“You aren’t something,” he replied, and his hand found hers almost
absentmindedly, fingers sliding between hers as though they’d always
been meant to rest there. “You’re Rachel. The rest of it is noise.”

She closed her eyes briefly at the contact, leaning into him without
meaning to.

“I thought if I kept it contained,” she admitted, “if I kept it smaller,
maybe it would stay manageable. Maybe they wouldn’t notice the
difference as much.”

“And now?”

“Now it doesn’t feel small anymore.”

He rested his forehead lightly against hers, not making a spectacle of
it, just anchoring them both.

“I was afraid,” he confessed quietly. “Not of you. Of losing you in it.”

She tightened her grip around his hand.

“I’m still here,” she said.

“For now,” he replied, not as a warning, but as acknowledgment of how
much had shifted.

“For now,” she echoed.

They stood like that as the square settled fully into night, lantern
light flickering against the repaired stone. Across the cobbles, the
braced pillar gave a faint creak as it adjusted to its supports.

Rachel drew in a slow breath, feeling the town’s uneven rhythm beneath
her feet.

“I don’t want to leave,” she said at last.

Arathen didn’t answer immediately. He only tightened his fingers around
hers.

“Then don’t,” he said.

And the town, cautious and altered and still standing, continued to
breathe around them.
