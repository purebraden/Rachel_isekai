# Chapter 26 — The Summons

The first sign was not the riders.

It was the way the square forgot what it had been doing.

A hammer paused mid-strike near the smithy. A cart wheel creaked once
and then went still. Someone at the well laughed at something half-said
and then stopped, the sound thinning into nothing as three horses came
into view along the eastern road.

No banners.

No herald.

Just the rhythm of hooves striking stone with steady precision.

Vaelis was already waiting in the square, coat formal, seal at his
collar catching the light. He stood not in the center of Thornhaven’s
space but slightly forward of it, positioned as intermediary rather than
host. He did not glance toward Rachel when she drifted closer to the
gathered townsfolk. He did not glance toward anyone.

He belonged to the Council first.

The first rider dismounted with unhurried control. Councilor Meredin was
tall, silver threaded at his temples, his coat marked with faint
Stabilist embroidery visible only when the fabric shifted. His gaze
moved across the square not with suspicion but with inventory.

The second rider swung down more sharply. Councilor Rethan removed his
gloves without looking at them, folding them once before tucking them
away. His eyes did not linger; they counted.

The third rider lingered half a breath longer in the saddle before
descending. Councilor Halvare folded his gloves carefully and slipped
them into his belt. He did not survey the square.

He looked at Rachel.

“Councilor Meredin,” Vaelis said, inclining his head.

“Vaelis.”

Recognition. Nothing theatrical.

Rethan’s gaze shifted past Vaelis almost immediately.

“Which one?” he asked.

A murmur rippled outward before anyone meant it to.

Sivara leaned slightly toward Rachel. “Rethan presses before he greets,”
she murmured.

“Rachel,” Vaelis said.

Halvare’s attention sharpened, not in accusation, but in focus.

Meredin’s gaze followed — Rachel, then Arathen, then Sivara, then the
town behind them.

“Thornhaven,” he said, acknowledging the space.

A few heads bowed. Others did not.

Rethan stepped forward before the square could settle.

“Your dispatch referenced unsanctioned aperture modulation,” he began,
but the sentence broke briefly as someone near the fountain shifted a
crate too roughly and wood scraped hard against stone.

He did not raise his voice.

“Clarify the deviation.”

Vaelis answered as record.

“The reinforcement hinge inverted under apprentice pressure. Rachel
altered the collapse vector rather than sealing conventionally.”

“Altered how?” Meredin asked before the explanation could complete.

Rachel felt the square tilt toward her.

“I adjusted the angle,” she said. “It was already destabilizing. Sealing
would have fractured the stone outward.”

“Without formal instruction in realignment protocol,” Rethan inserted.

“Yes.”

Someone near the well muttered, “There is no protocol,” and Maelin
turned sharply enough that the whisper thinned.

Halvare had not looked toward the speaker. He was watching Rachel’s
hands, which she hadn’t realized were flexing faintly at her sides, as
though remembering pressure.

“Has this adjustment been incorporated into training?” he asked.

Sivara answered without hesitation.

“No.”

“Has she demonstrated it to apprentices?”

“She has demonstrated correction in active collapse,” Sivara said. “Not
as curriculum.”

“And apprentices have observed,” Rethan said.

“They have observed collapse,” Sivara replied.

“They have observed her.”

The distinction lingered.

At the edge of the square, Caelen shifted his weight, the movement
visible even through the press of people. Someone stepped aside to give
him clearer sight. He did not ask for it.

“And the apprentice,” Rethan continued. “Caelen. Did he attempt
replication after observing this method?”

“He acted without instruction,” Sivara said.

“That is not the same answer.”

“It is the relevant one.”

A low stir moved outward — not protest, not quite agreement.

Halvare stepped half a pace closer to Rachel.

“You said the distortion hesitated,” he said, his tone shifting — less
procedural, more intent. “Have you encountered one prior to this
incident?”

Rachel hesitated — not long, but long enough for the square to feel it.

“Yes.”

“How many times?” Rethan asked immediately, but someone behind her
whispered “Three?” at the same moment, as though guessing.

Rachel blinked.

“Three,” she said.

The number settled differently than the others.

A child was drawn closer to a parent. Ilyra shifted her stance. Arathen
did not move.

“Unreported?” Meredin asked.

“The prior engagement did not manifest publicly,” Vaelis said.

“What occurred,” Halvare asked Rachel directly, “when it formed?”

She closed her eyes briefly, just long enough to feel for the shape of
it.

“It didn’t attack,” she said. “It watched.”

“Watched,” Rethan repeated.

“It assessed,” Rachel corrected, surprising herself with the sharpness
of the word.

A woman near the bakery adjusted her shawl higher on her shoulders as
though a chill had passed through her.

“Has its behavior altered across engagements?” Meredin asked.

“Yes.”

“Increasing complexity?” Halvare asked.

Rachel nodded.

“Escalation,” Rethan said.

“Adaptation,” Halvare replied at the same moment.

“Anthropomorphism.”

“Pattern recognition.”

Their voices overlapped, not raised but layered, and the fracture
between them showed plainly now.

“Have you attempted to avoid engagement?” Meredin asked.

“Yes.”

“And?”

“It formed anyway.”

The admission shifted the air.

Rethan’s boots scraped softly against stone as he adjusted his stance.

“You cannot choose when it forms.”

“No.”

Someone behind Rachel stepped back half a pace, as though the answer
required space.

“Containment measures?” Rethan asked Vaelis.

“Standard reinforcement,” Vaelis replied. “No cascade.”

“And recurrence persists.”

“If convergence is architectural—” Halvare began.

“—then recurrence is inevitable,” Rethan finished.

“—then avoidance is irrelevant,” Halvare corrected almost
simultaneously.

They stopped speaking at the same moment, tension drawn tight but
contained.

A breeze caught Meredin’s coat and lifted the hem before letting it fall
again.

“If the archive is silent,” Halvare said quietly, almost thoughtfully,
“it may not be because nothing was written.”

Rethan’s gaze sharpened.

“Speculation.”

“Possibility.”

“Risk.”

“Data.”

The square felt suddenly crowded with breath.

Meredin let the silence stretch longer than comfort allowed.

“If terminology cannot be verified,” he said at last, “and recurrence
cannot be prevented, then assessment cannot remain theoretical.”

No one interrupted that.

Rethan stepped into the opening.

“Then we observe it.”

Halvare inclined his head.

“Under structure.”

“Under oversight.”

“Publicly,” Meredin added, and the word drew immediate reaction — a
flinch, a muttered objection, someone beginning to protest before
Ilyra’s sharp look quieted it.

Vaelis did not speak at once.

He watched the square.

He watched Rachel.

Then he inclined his head.

“Preparation will be made.”

No one said the word demonstration.

It did not need to be said.

Across the square, someone had already begun dragging the long
reinforcement table toward the center, as though the decision had been
waiting for a pause in conversation to begin moving. Wood scraped
against stone. Chalk appeared without instruction.

“They’ll want clean lines,” Ilyra said, already calculating distance.

“Not too close to the well,” Sivara murmured. “We don’t want fracture
beneath the stones.”

Caelen stood pale at the edge, his arm bound against his chest, watching
without blinking.

Rachel hadn’t realized her hands were clenched until her fingers ached.

The center of the square stood empty now.

Waiting.

She stepped forward before she fully meant to.

The cobblestones felt different beneath her boots.
